\section{Theory}

An electrical circuit consisting of a resistor and a capacitor---commonly 
referred to as an RC circuit---exhibits a fundamentally different behavior 
from a purely resistive circuit. At the heart of this difference is the 
capacitor's ability to store charge on its plates. For a capacitor subjected 
to an electric potential difference $V$, the relationship between the stored 
charge $Q$ and the voltage is given by:

\begin{align*}
    Q = CV
\end{align*}

\noindent where $C$ is the capacitance, a property of the capacitor that 
quantifies how much charge it can store per unit voltage. What makes RC 
circuits particularly interesting is not just the fact that charge accumulates, 
but \textit{how} it accumulates over time.

When a switch is closed and the capacitor begins to charge, the rate at which 
charge builds up on the plates is not constant---it slows down as the voltage 
across the capacitor approaches that of the power supply. This behavior is 
characteristic of an exponential function, a special class of function whose 
rate of change is proportional to its current value. In other words, the more 
charge the capacitor has already accumulated, the slower it continues to 
charge. This same logic applies in reverse during discharging: the more charge 
remains, the faster it leaves, and the process slows as the plates approach 
neutrality.

To see where this exponential form comes from, we can apply Kirchhoff's 
voltage law to the RC circuit during charging. The voltage supplied by the 
source $\mathcal{E}$ must equal the sum of the voltage drops across the 
resistor and the capacitor:

\begin{align*}
    \mathcal{E} = V_R + V_C = IR + \frac{Q}{C}
\end{align*}

\noindent Since the current is the rate of change of charge, $I = dQ/dt$, 
we can substitute directly into the equation above to obtain the following 
first-order differential equation:

\begin{align*}
    \mathcal{E} = R\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{Q}{C}
\end{align*}

\noindent Rearranging to isolate the derivative:

\begin{align*}
    \frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} - \frac{Q}{RC}
\end{align*}

\noindent This equation tells us that the rate at which charge accumulates 
on the capacitor decreases as $Q$ grows---precisely the behavior we 
described as exponential. Solving this differential equation with the 
initial condition $Q(0) = 0$, and converting back to voltage using 
$V_C = Q/C$, yields the solution for the voltage across the capacitor 
during charging:

\begin{align*}
    V_C(t) = \mathcal{E}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)
\end{align*}

\noindent and during discharging from an initial voltage $V_0$:

\begin{align*}
    V_C(t) = V_0\, e^{-t/\tau}
\end{align*}

\noindent In both expressions, $\tau$ is the \textbf{RC time constant}, 
defined as:

\begin{align*}
    \tau = RC
\end{align*}

\noindent The time constant $\tau$ carries units of seconds and has a concrete 
physical meaning: it is the time it takes for the capacitor to charge to 
approximately $63.2\%$ of the supply voltage, or to discharge to approximately 
$36.8\%$ of its initial voltage---values that follow directly from evaluating 
$e^{-1}$.

The goal of this experiment is to verify this theoretical prediction 
experimentally using an RC circuit built on a breadboard with a known 
resistance $R = 3{,}300\ \Omega$ and capacitance $C = 470\ \mu\text{F}$, 
giving a theoretical time constant of:

\begin{align*}
    \tau_{\text{theory}} = RC = (3{,}300)(470 \times 10^{-6}) \approx 1.551\ 
    \text{s}
\end{align*}

\noindent Rather than measuring charge or current directly, we record the 
voltage across the capacitor over time using the PASCO 850 interface and 
voltage sensor, as voltage is the most directly accessible quantity given our 
available instrumentation.

\section*{Conclusion}

\subsection*{Activity 1: Charging a Capacitor}

To evaluate the charging behavior of the capacitor, the voltage data
collected from the PASCO sensor was fitted to an exponential trendline
in Excel. Since the charging equation takes the form
$V_C(t) = \mathcal{E}(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, the coefficient $-1/RC$ was
used as the key quantity to compare between theory and experiment, as
it can be extracted directly from the exponential trendline fit. The
theoretical and experimental values are summarized below:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{Theoretical} & \textbf{Experimental} \\
\midrule
$-1/RC$ (s$^{-1}$) & $-0.644745$ & $-0.514350$ \\
Percent Error       & \multicolumn{2}{c}{$20.22\%$} \\
$R^2$               & \multicolumn{2}{c}{$0.99829$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The $R^2$ value of $0.99829$ and a percent error of $20.22\%$, well
within our acceptable threshold of $25\%$, confirm that the theoretical
model successfully predicts the exponential charging behavior of the
capacitor. The voltage across the capacitor rises in a manner consistent
with the form prescribed by theory, validating the RC model for this
activity.

\subsection*{Activity 2: Discharging a Capacitor}

For the discharging phase, the capacitor was allowed to charge fully
before the power supply was switched off, and the subsequent voltage
decay was recorded and fitted in the same manner. The discharge follows
$V_C(t) = V_0\, e^{-t/\tau}$, and the results are summarized below:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{Theoretical} & \textbf{Experimental} \\
\midrule
$-1/RC$ (s$^{-1}$) & $-0.644745$ & $-0.564400$ \\
Percent Error       & \multicolumn{2}{c}{$14.23\%$} \\
$R^2$               & \multicolumn{2}{c}{$0.99974$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The $R^2$ value of $0.99974$ and a percent error of $14.23\%$ confirm
once again that the theoretical model successfully predicts the
exponential discharging behavior of the capacitor. The voltage decays
in a manner fully consistent with the theoretical form, further
validating the RC model for this activity.

\subsection*{Overall Conclusion}

Across both activities, the RC model proves to be a reliable predictor
of the voltage behavior of a capacitor, with both percent errors
falling well within the $25\%$ acceptable threshold and $R^2$ values
approaching unity. However, a notable observation emerges when comparing
the two experimentally obtained values of $-1/RC$: the charging activity
yielded $-0.514350$ s$^{-1}$, while the discharging activity yielded
$-0.564400$ s$^{-1}$. These two values are not equal, despite the fact
that the same physical components were used in both cases. This
discrepancy points to a real limitation of the setup---during charging,
the PASCO 850 power supply is active in the circuit and its internal
resistance adds to the total resistance experienced by the capacitor,
effectively slowing the charging rate and pushing the experimental
$-1/RC$ further from the theoretical value. During discharging, the
power supply is disconnected, and so its internal resistance no longer
participates, allowing the capacitor to discharge more closely to the
ideal prediction.

To address this limitation in future experiments, we propose computing
the theoretical $RC$ using the actual voltage the capacitor experiences
at the onset of each phase, rather than relying on the nominal component
values alone. This approach would decouple the charging and discharging
dynamics from the influence of the power supply, producing a theoretical
prediction that is tailored to each activity independently and therefore
more physically meaningful.

\section*{Above and Beyond}

One of the most quietly profound observations of this experiment is not
found in the data itself, but in the tool we used to describe it. The
exponential function, a construct born purely from the rules of calculus
and the study of self-referential rates of change, turns out to describe
with remarkable precision the physical behavior of electrons accumulating
on a capacitor plate. This is not obvious. There is no immediate reason
why the abstract solution to a differential equation should map so cleanly
onto the behavior of a circuit sitting on a breadboard. And yet it does.

This alignment between mathematics and physical reality has puzzled
philosophers for centuries. When negative numbers were first introduced,
they were met with deep skepticism---the idea of a quantity less than
nothing seemed to have no grounding in the real world, a purely
symbolic trick with no physical referent. Imaginary numbers faced an
even steeper resistance: Gerolamo Cardano, who first made use of them
in the 16th century, described them as ``fictitious'' and ``useless.''
And yet imaginary numbers today are indispensable in the very field this
experiment touches---AC circuit analysis, quantum mechanics, and signal
processing all rest on a foundation built from $\sqrt{-1}$. Linear
algebra, once regarded as an abstract exercise in manipulating arrays of
numbers, became the backbone of computer graphics, machine learning, and
search algorithms. In each case, the mathematics preceded the application
by decades or centuries, waiting quietly until the physical world caught
up and revealed where it had been hiding all along.

The physicist Eugene Wigner famously called this phenomenon ``the
unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics''---the strange and
unexplained fact that mathematical structures developed with no applied
purpose in mind consistently turn out to describe nature with
extraordinary precision. It raises a question that remains genuinely
open: is mathematics discovered or invented? Are we uncovering a
structure that was always embedded in the universe, or are we
building a language so flexible that it inevitably finds a way to fit
whatever we point it at?

What mathematical abstractions are still waiting for their physical
moment? Topology, the study of properties preserved under continuous
deformation, was considered almost entirely theoretical until it found
a home in the classification of phases of matter, earning the 2016
Nobel Prize in Physics. Quaternions, a generalization of complex numbers
invented by William Hamilton in 1843, were largely abandoned by
physicists for over a century before re-emerging as the preferred tool
for describing rotations in three-dimensional space---now used in every
modern video game engine and spacecraft orientation system. Number
theory, long considered the purest and most application-free branch of
mathematics, became the foundation of modern cryptography and internet
security.

This experiment and its simple exponential function serve as a reminder that the
world and the technology around us are built on these mathematical foundations.
From the circuits in our devices to the signals they transmit, abstract
mathematics quietly underpins the physical reality we interact with every day.
It leaves us to wonder what other connections between nature and mathematics are
still waiting to be realized.
