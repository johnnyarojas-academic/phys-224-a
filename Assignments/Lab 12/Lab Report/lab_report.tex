\section{Theory}

An electrical circuit consisting of a resistor and a capacitor---commonly 
referred to as an RC circuit---exhibits a fundamentally different behavior 
from a purely resistive circuit. At the heart of this difference is the 
capacitor's ability to store charge on its plates. For a capacitor subjected 
to an electric potential difference $V$, the relationship between the stored 
charge $Q$ and the voltage is given by:

\begin{align*}
    Q = CV
\end{align*}

\noindent where $C$ is the capacitance, a property of the capacitor that 
quantifies how much charge it can store per unit voltage. What makes RC 
circuits particularly interesting is not just the fact that charge accumulates, 
but \textit{how} it accumulates over time.

When a switch is closed and the capacitor begins to charge, the rate at which 
charge builds up on the plates is not constant---it slows down as the voltage 
across the capacitor approaches that of the power supply. This behavior is 
characteristic of an exponential function, a special class of function whose 
rate of change is proportional to its current value. In other words, the more 
charge the capacitor has already accumulated, the slower it continues to 
charge. This same logic applies in reverse during discharging: the more charge 
remains, the faster it leaves, and the process slows as the plates approach 
neutrality.

To see where this exponential form comes from, we can apply Kirchhoff's 
voltage law to the RC circuit during charging. The voltage supplied by the 
source $\mathcal{E}$ must equal the sum of the voltage drops across the 
resistor and the capacitor:

\begin{align*}
    \mathcal{E} = V_R + V_C = IR + \frac{Q}{C}
\end{align*}

\noindent Since the current is the rate of change of charge, $I = dQ/dt$, 
we can substitute directly into the equation above to obtain the following 
first-order differential equation:

\begin{align*}
    \mathcal{E} = R\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{Q}{C}
\end{align*}

\noindent Rearranging to isolate the derivative:

\begin{align*}
    \frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} - \frac{Q}{RC}
\end{align*}

\noindent This equation tells us that the rate at which charge accumulates 
on the capacitor decreases as $Q$ grows---precisely the behavior we 
described as exponential. Solving this differential equation with the 
initial condition $Q(0) = 0$, and converting back to voltage using 
$V_C = Q/C$, yields the solution for the voltage across the capacitor 
during charging:

\begin{align*}
    V_C(t) = \mathcal{E}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)
\end{align*}

\noindent and during discharging from an initial voltage $V_0$:

\begin{align*}
    V_C(t) = V_0\, e^{-t/\tau}
\end{align*}

\noindent In both expressions, $\tau$ is the \textbf{RC time constant}, 
defined as:

\begin{align*}
    \tau = RC
\end{align*}

\noindent The time constant $\tau$ carries units of seconds and has a concrete 
physical meaning: it is the time it takes for the capacitor to charge to 
approximately $63.2\%$ of the supply voltage, or to discharge to approximately 
$36.8\%$ of its initial voltage---values that follow directly from evaluating 
$e^{-1}$.

The goal of this experiment is to verify this theoretical prediction 
experimentally using an RC circuit built on a breadboard with a known 
resistance $R = 3{,}300\ \Omega$ and capacitance $C = 470\ \mu\text{F}$, 
giving a theoretical time constant of:

\begin{align*}
    \tau_{\text{theory}} = RC = (3{,}300)(470 \times 10^{-6}) \approx 1.551\ 
    \text{s}
\end{align*}

\noindent Rather than measuring charge or current directly, we record the 
voltage across the capacitor over time using the PASCO 850 interface and 
voltage sensor, as voltage is the most directly accessible quantity given our 
available instrumentation.
