* Overview

The goal is to derive $\int_1^x \frac{1}{t} \, dt$ from scratch, using
nothing but the Binomial Theorem and the definition of the integral as a
Riemann sum. The result will naturally produce the logarithm, and we will
see /why/ $\ln(x)$ is the right name for this function.

The road map is:

1. *Binomial Theorem* — expand $(1 + u)^n$ for any real exponent.
2. *Riemann sum for* $\int_1^x t^n \, dt$ — integrate a monomial by summing
   rectangles and taking a limit.
3. *Set* $n = -1$ — watch what goes wrong and how to fix it.
4. *Recover the sum* — the fix produces the series for $\ln(x)$ and
   identifies the integral with the logarithm.

-----

* 1. The Binomial Theorem

** Statement

For Any real number $r$ and $|u| < 1$:

\begin{align*}
  (1 + u)^r
  &= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \binom{r}{k} u^k \\
  &= 1 + ru + \frac{r(r-1)}{2!}u^2 + \frac{r(r-1)(r-2)}{3!}u^3 + \cdots
\end{align*}

where the generalised binomial coefficient is defined as:

\begin{align*}
  \binom{r}{k} = \frac{r(r-1)(r-2)\cdots(r-k+1)}{k!}
\end{align*}

This extends the familiar integer-exponent binomial theorem to /any/ real
(or even complex) exponent $r$.

** The case we need: $r = n$, a positive integer first

For a positive integer $n$ the series terminates and gives the familiar
polynomial. This is what lets us integrate $t^n$ cleanly. The case $r = -1$
is where the story gets interesting.

-----

* 2. Integrating a Monomial: $\int_1^x t^n \, dt$

** Riemann sum setup

Partition the interval $[1, x]$ into $N$ subintervals. To keep the algebra
clean, use a *geometric partition* — points spaced by a constant ratio
$\rho = x^{1/N}$ rather than a constant width:

\begin{align*}
  t_k = \rho^k, \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, N, \qquad \rho = x^{1/N}
\end{align*}

The width of the $k$-th subinterval is:

\begin{align*}
  \Delta t_k = t_k - t_{k-1} = \rho^k - \rho^{k-1} = \rho^{k-1}(\rho - 1)
\end{align*}

Using the left endpoint $t_{k-1} = \rho^{k-1}$ to evaluate $f(t) = t^n$,
the Riemann sum is:

\begin{align*}
  S_N
  = \sum_{k=1}^{N} t_{k-1}^n \cdot \Delta t_k
  = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \rho^{(k-1)n} \cdot \rho^{k-1}(\rho - 1)
  = (\rho - 1) \sum_{k=1}^{N} \rho^{(k-1)(n+1)}
\end{align*}

** Evaluating the geometric series

The sum is a geometric series with ratio $\rho^{n+1}$:

\begin{align*}
  \sum_{k=1}^{N} \rho^{(k-1)(n+1)}
  = \frac{\rho^{N(n+1)} - 1}{\rho^{n+1} - 1}
  = \frac{x^{n+1} - 1}{\rho^{n+1} - 1}
\end{align*}

since $\rho^N = x$. So the Riemann sum becomes:

\begin{align*}
  S_N = (\rho - 1) \cdot \frac{x^{n+1} - 1}{\rho^{n+1} - 1}
\end{align*}

** Taking the limit $N \to \infty$ (i.e. $\rho \to 1$)

As $N \to \infty$, $\rho = x^{1/N} \to 1$. Apply L'Hôpital (or the
standard limit $\lim_{u \to 0} \frac{u}{e^u - 1} = 1$ with $u = \ln\rho$):

\begin{align*}
  \lim_{\rho \to 1} \frac{\rho - 1}{\rho^{n+1} - 1} = \frac{1}{n+1}
\end{align*}

Therefore:

\begin{align*}
  \int_1^x t^n \, dt = \frac{x^{n+1} - 1}{n+1}, \qquad n \neq -1
\end{align*}

This is exactly the power rule. Note the denominator $n+1$ — it blows up
when $n = -1$, which is precisely the case we want.

-----

* 3. The Problem at $n = -1$

Plugging $n = -1$ into the formula gives $\frac{x^0 - 1}{0} = \frac{0}{0}$,
which is undefined. The power rule has a gap exactly at $\frac{1}{t}$.

But the Riemann sum is still well-defined! Go back to it with $n = -1$:

\begin{align*}
  S_N
  = (\rho - 1) \cdot \frac{x^{0} - 1}{\rho^{0} - 1}
\end{align*}

This is also $\frac{0}{0}$, but only because we substituted $\rho^N = x$
too early. Before that substitution the sum with $n = -1$ is:

\begin{align*}
  S_N
  = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \rho^{(k-1)(-1)} \cdot \rho^{k-1}(\rho - 1)
  = \sum_{k=1}^{N} (\rho - 1)
  = N(\rho - 1)
\end{align*}

Every $\rho$-power cancels. The sum collapses to the beautifully simple
expression $N(\rho - 1)$.

-----

* 4. Evaluating $N(\rho - 1)$ as $N \to \infty$

** The key limit

With $\rho = x^{1/N}$ we have:

\begin{align*}
  N(\rho - 1) = N\!\left(x^{1/N} - 1\right)
\end{align*}

Let $u = \frac{\ln x}{N}$, so $x^{1/N} = e^u$ and $u \to 0$ as
$N \to \infty$:

\begin{align*}
  N(x^{1/N} - 1)
  = \frac{e^u - 1}{u} \cdot \ln x
  \xrightarrow{u \to 0} 1 \cdot \ln x = \ln x
\end{align*}

using the standard limit $\lim_{u \to 0} \frac{e^u - 1}{u} = 1$.

** Result

\begin{align*}
  \boxed{\int_1^x \frac{1}{t} \, dt = \ln x}
\end{align*}

The integral exists and equals $\ln x$ — the power rule's gap is filled by
the logarithm.

-----

* 5. Connecting to the Binomial Series

The Binomial Theorem gives us an independent path to the same answer via a
series. Write $\frac{1}{t} = \frac{1}{1-(1-t)}$ and expand near $t = 1$.
More directly, substitute $t = 1 + u$ and expand $\frac{1}{1+u}$ using
$r = -1$ in the Binomial Theorem:

\begin{align*}
  \frac{1}{1+u}
  = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (-1)^k u^k
  = 1 - u + u^2 - u^3 + \cdots, \qquad |u| < 1
\end{align*}

Integrate term by term from $0$ to $x - 1$ (i.e. from $t=1$ to $t=x$,
setting $u = t - 1$):

\begin{align*}
  \int_1^x \frac{1}{t}\,dt
  &= \int_0^{x-1} \frac{1}{1+u}\,du \\
  &= \int_0^{x-1} \left(1 - u + u^2 - u^3 + \cdots\right) du \\
  &= \left[u - \frac{u^2}{2} + \frac{u^3}{3} - \frac{u^4}{4} + \cdots\right]_0^{x-1}
\end{align*}

Setting $v = x - 1$:

\begin{align*}
  \int_1^x \frac{1}{t}\,dt
  = v - \frac{v^2}{2} + \frac{v^3}{3} - \cdots
  = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}(x-1)^k
\end{align*}

This is exactly the Taylor series for $\ln(x)$ around $x = 1$, confirming
both paths agree.

-----

* 6. Why the Logarithm?

It is worth pausing to note /why/ this function deserves to be called a
logarithm. Define:

\begin{align*}
  L(x) = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t}\,dt
\end{align*}

One can show directly from the integral (by a change of variables $t \mapsto
at$) that:

\begin{align*}
  L(ab) = L(a) + L(b)
\end{align*}

This is the defining property of a logarithm. The base $e$ is the unique
number satisfying $L(e) = 1$, i.e.:

\begin{align*}
  \int_1^e \frac{1}{t}\,dt = 1
\end{align*}

So $L(x) = \ln x$ is not a coincidence — the integral /is/ the logarithm,
by definition.
